Abstract 

Split learning has emerged as a promising paradigm for privacy-preserving LLM fine-tuning, yet its practical deployment is severely hindered by the sequential communication-computation bottleneck. In conventional synchronous pipelines, clients remain idle while waiting for server-side gradients, resulting in substantial training inefficiency. We propose CommuteProp, an asynchronous split-learning algorithm that decouples the training process into two concurrent phases: a cross-block forward-backward pass and an in-block weight update. By overlapping computation with communication, CommuteProp reduces the marginal cycle time from a sum of all stage latencies to the dominant computational bottleneck. We provide a rigorous asynchronous error and convergence analysis. Moreover, we derive an NS preconditioner method based on our analysis to further mitigate staleness-induced noise. Comprehensive experiments indicate that our algorithm achieves substantial throughput gains while maintaining accuracy comparable to synchronous methods; furthermore, it functions as a plug-and-play module that not only accommodates but actively enhances existing privacy enhancement methods. 

# 1 Introduction 

The increasing demand for fine-tuning Large Language Models (LLMs)(Vaswani et al., 2017) on proprietary datasets has encountered a fundamental conflict between model utility and data privacy. In highly regulated sectors such as healthcare and finance, raw data is highly regulated from leaving local custody due to stringent privacy protocolsVoigt and Von dem Bussche (2017). While Federated Learning (FL) (Kairouz and McMahan, 2021) offers a decentralized alternative, the colossal memory requirements of LLMs often exceed the hardware limits of local clients, rendering full-model local training infeasible. Consequently, Split Learning (SL) (Gupta and Raskar, 2018) has emerged as a critical paradigm. By partitioning the model across institutional boundaries, SL allows clients to retain sensitive data locally while offloading the memory-intensive computation to a powerful server. Specifically, let the model F be partitioned into N layers with parameters θ1, . . . , θ N , let D be the data distribution with samples (x, y), and let Ltask denote the loss function. We have the following mathematical problem:

Although SL offers a promising privacy-preserving paradigm, it is severely bottlenecked by substantial communication overhead. In the conventional synchronous execution, the client remains idle during the server-side computation and the subsequent backpropagation of gradients. Specifically, let tclient and tserver denote the local processing times for the client and server, respectively, and tc represent the one-way communication latency. While asynchrony inevitably introduces gradient staleness, we provide a rigorous convergence analysis for CommuteProp. Our results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm signifi-cantly improves training throughput while maintaining competitive model accuracy. Moreover, we provide the ablation study, further shows the impact of gradient staleness on convergence stability, providing insights into the trade-off between asynchrony and optimization trajectory. Furthermore, consistent with recent work Liu et al. (2025) that introduces privacy-preserving methods in SL, our experiment indicates that CommuteProp, as a plug-and-play framework, not only accommodates but actively enhances existing privacy enhancement methods. We summarize the contribution of this paper as follows: (1) We propose CommuteProp, an asynchronous split-learning algorithm that overlaps computation and communication via a two-phase pipeline; (2) We propose a rigorous asynchronous error and convergence analysis. We also introduce the NS preconditioner method based on our analysis to reduce the asynchronous error; (3) Comprehensive experiments on NLP benchmarks demonstrate substantial throughput gains, competitive accuracy, and plug-and-play compatibility with privacy-enhancing methods. 

# 2 Related Works 

SL in LLM Fine-Tuning. Split Learning (SL) Gupta and Raskar (2018); Vepakomma et al. (2018) partitions a model across client and server, allowing the client to offload heavy computation while retaining only a few layers locally. U-Shaped Split Learning (USL) Gupta and Raskar (2018); Lyu et al. (2023) further 2assigns the head and tail to the client, with the trunk delegated to the server. This architecture is well-suited for autoregressive LLMs. However, recent work Gu et al. (2025); Chen et al. (2024) shows that SL remains vulnerable to data reconstruction attacks, allowing servers to recover portions of the client’s private data. This has led to defense mechanisms Liu et al. (2025); Gu et al. (2026) designed to mitigate such risks and strengthen the training process. 

Pipeline Parallelism. SL is structurally similar to Pipeline Parallelism (PP). In HPC settings, synchronous PP with micro-batching achieves high throughput and negligible communication overhead Harlap et al. (2018); Qi et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2024). When communication is costly, asynchronous alternatives become preferable, but they suffer from gradient staleness and often require storing historical weights, an impractical storage requirement in SL. Solutions like approximate weight updates Yang et al. (2021) and Nesterov Ajanthan et al. (2025) have been proposed; however, they have only been tested on small models (e.g., nanoGPT Karpathy (2022)). In this work, we adopt synchronous training and the Nesterov-based(without Weight Stashing) method Ajanthan et al. (2025) as baselines, and scale the evaluation to Qwen2.5-1.5B while providing convergence guarantees.

.33.2 CommuteProp Framework 

Under the two-phase Gauss-Seidel optimization scheme discussed above, each block in SL must perform three distinct tasks: in Phase 1, (i) receiving outputs from the preceding block, computing and transmitting the activations hl+1 to the subsequent block during forward propagation; (ii) receiving gradients from the subse-quent block, computing and transmitting the gradients gl+1 to the preceding block during backpropagation; and in Phase 2, (iii) performing the in-block update of parameters θl.Motivated by this analysis, we propose CommuteProp (illustrated in Figure 1), a framework that processes these tasks asynchronously across different data batches. Because the Phase 2 parameter updates rely on the gradients gl+1 computed during Phase 1, we establish two separate queues, Q1 and Q2, for each block. Phase 1 tasks are managed in Q1, while the in-block parameter update tasks (Phase 2) are put in Q2. These queues store only the essential contextual information required for each task, such as hl and gl+1 . To strictly control memory consumption, we cap the maximum length of each queue at τmax . Furthermore, we exclude the parameter weights θl from the queued task information, as they typically possess a significantly larger memory footprint than either the activations or their gradients. To mitigate this, inspired by the Muon optimizer in first-order orthogonalization, we propose to explicitly 

whiten the gradient space prior to applying the diagonal operator Mj . By introducing a preconditioner 7Pj ≈ H−1/2 

> j,j

, we transform the unbounded inter-block covariance into a bounded correlation operator, thereby neutralizing the asynchronous error. To achieve this inverse square root approximation efficiently without heavy eigendecomposition, we introduce the Kronecker-Block Newton-Schulz (NS) Preconditioner. For 2D parameter tensors (e.g., weight matrices), let U ∈ RM ×N denote the reshaped gradient matrix of the block update egl, as detailed in Algorithm 3. To ensure efficiency, Algorithm 3 employs a Kronecker-factored structure (inspired by SOAPVyas et al. (2024)) to tractably approximate high-dimensional second-moment statistics without materializing the full Hessian. Furthermore, since the empirical Fisher information evolves slowly during training Zhao et al. (2024), we implement lazy updates for both covariance aggregation and preconditioner generation. This spatio-temporal design significantly amortizes computational overhead while strictly preserving the spatial decorrelation crucial for robust asynchronous decoupling.

We proposed CommuteProp, an asynchronous split-learning algorithm that decouples training into cross-block and in-block phases to overlap communication with computation. By exploiting the inherently small cross-block Hessian of LLMs, we theoretically proved that our algorithm is resistant to asynchronous errors, and we accordingly introduced the NS preconditioner to further mitigate residual noise. Experiments show that CommuteProp achieves substantial throughput gains, maintains accuracy, and can integrate with or even enhance existing privacy-preserving methods. Notably, our theoretical analysis is not confined to Transformers and can be applied to feedforward networks as well. With an appropriate partition, the cross-block Hessian norm could remain bounded, thus carrying over the convergence guarantees. However, identifying an optimal splitting strategy for feedforward architectures remains challenging, because they lack the shortcut mechanism that naturally induces small inter-block interaction. Naive splitting may inflate the cross-block Hessian and break our assumptions. We consider the design of principled splitting rules for general architectures as a meaningful direction for future work.

# B Implementation Details 

## B.1 Model and Split Configuration 

We fine-tune the Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct model Hui et al. (2024). The U-shaped split architecture is denoted by a triplet [nhead , n bulk , n tail ] giving the number of Transformer blocks on the client head, server bulk, and client tail, respectively. Throughout Section 5.2 we use the default split [3 , 22 , 3] . All methods employ a batch size of 16 with a cosine learning rate schedule. 

## B.2 Optimizer Details 

Synchronous AdamW (baseline). We use the standard PyTorch implementation of AdamW Loshchilov and Hutter (2017) with β1=0 .9, β 2=0 .999 , weight decay 0.09 , and ϵ=1 × 10 −8. The base learning rate is set to η=2 × 10 −5 in the main experiments. 

CommuteProp and CommuteProp (NS). CommuteProp wraps any base optimizer; the same AdamW hyperparameters serve the CommuteProp (AdamW) variant. The staleness delay τ is emulated via a queue-based simulator: a gradient update is triggered only after the corresponding forward activation has been queued for exactly τ micro-batches. For the utility comparison (Table 1) we set τ =16 . The “NS” variant applies the Kronecker-Block Newton–Schulz preconditioner (Algorithm 3) before the AdamW step; we use a block size b=64 and refresh the preconditioner every 200 steps. 

Nesterov Async. We re-implement the asynchronous pipeline parallel method of Ajanthan et al. (2025) with its default momentum factor 0.9 and Nesterov correction; the staleness delay is fixed at τ =16 for the fairness comparison. 

Synchronous Muon (baseline). We use the standard PyTorch implementation of Muon Jordan et al. (2024) with β1=0 .9, β 2=0 .999 , weight decay 0.09 , and ϵ=1 × 10 −8. The base learning rate is set to η=2 × 10 −5

in the main experiments. 

CommuteProp (Muon). The “Muon” variant replaces AdamW with the Muon optimizer Jordan et al. (2024) (momentum 0.95 , Nesterov acceleration enabled) while keeping the CommuteProp two-phase pipeline unchanged. 

## B.3 Full-Training Comparison and Iso-time Comparison 

In our experiments we report both full-training and iso-time comparisons. The full-training comparison verifies the utility of CommuteProp in end-to-end training, while the iso-time comparison, controlled by equal wall-clock budgets, directly captures the efficiency gain in the split learning scenario. Our experiment use one single NVIDIA RTX A6000. For the iso-time comparison, we first profile the per-stage computation costs. We record the forward and backward times of the synchronous baseline (AdamW) and of CommuteProp (NS) over the first 50 training iterations and average the measurements. To isolate the speedup coming purely from computation-communication overlap, we exclude the time of optimizer updates, gradient zeroing, and learning rate scheduling, as these operations are nearly identical in both settings, non-overlappable, and would only add a constant offset to the end-to-end latency without affecting the relative gain from pipelining. ## B.6 Datasets and Evaluation 

We evaluate on four diverse NLP benchmarks: Emotion, AG-News, SAMSum, SciERC and Spider. Emo-tion Saravia et al. (2018) is a 6-class emotion classification dataset (sadness, joy, love, anger, fear, surprise) with 16,000 training samples and 2,000 test samples; texts are short social media posts with an average length of approximately 20 words. AG News Zhang et al. (2015) is a 4-class topic classification dataset (World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech) with 120,000 training samples and 7,600 test samples; texts are news articles averaging 40–50 words. SAMSum Gliwa et al. (2019) is a dialogue summarization dataset with 14,732 training samples and 819 test samples; each dialogue contains multiple turns of casual conversation, and the reference summaries average approximately 20 words. SciERC Luan et al. (2018) is a scientific relation extraction dataset with 3,219 training samples and 974 test samples; texts are scientific paper abstracts, and the task is to classify the relation between annotated entity pairs into one of 7 relation types (labels 0–6). Spider Yu et al. (2018) is a cross-domain text-to-SQL dataset with 7,000 training samples and 1,034 test samples; each example provides a natural language question paired with the corresponding SQL query spanning 200 databases. For all datasets, we report final results on the official test split. Emotion, AG-News and Spider are trained for 3 epochs, while SAMSum and SciERC are trained for 5 epochs. For preprocessing, we apply the Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct chat template to format each example into a consistent instruction-following format, and truncate all sequences to a maximum length of 2,048 tokens. For Spider, the instruction asks the model to translate the question into an SQL query. Padding is performed dynamically per batch, which also masks the user-instruction portion of each sequence in the loss computation using labels=-100 , so that the model is trained only on the assistant response tokens. Reported metrics are Accuracy (for classification), ROUGE-L and METEOR (for summarization), macro-F1 (for relation extraction), and exact-set-match (EM) accuracy (for text-to-SQL), consistent with prior work. For Emotion (6-class emotion classification) and AG-News (4-class topic classification), we compute accuracy as the fraction of samples where the model’s predicted label exactly matches the ground-truth label. For SAMSum (dialogue summarization), we report ROUGE-L F-measure as the primary metric and METEOR as a secondary metric; both are computed using the HuggingFace evaluate library with default settings (ROUGE-L with stemming enabled via use_stemmer=True ). For SciERC (scientific relation extraction with 7 relation types), we report macro-averaged F1 score across all 7 classes. For Spider, we report exact-set-match accuracy, which compares the execution results of the predicted SQL query against the gold query on the target database—a prediction is considered correct only if the two result sets are identical. During inference, we use greedy decoding ( do_sample=False ) with dataset-specific maximum generation lengths: 10 tokens for Emotion and AG-News (single-label output), 20 tokens for SciERC (single-digit output), 128 tokens for Spider (SQL query generation), and 128 tokens for SAMSum (summary generation). 16 B.7 Integration with DualGuard 

B.7.1 Introductions 

In Section 5.3. We integrate CommuteProp into the DualGuard framework Liu et al. (2025). DualGuard assumes that the adversaries can access to (1) the pre-trained model weights (publicly available), (2) the transmitted smashed data and gradients during training, and (3) an auxiliary dataset for training attack models. The attacker aims to reconstruct the private training data. Specifically, they introduce four attack mechanisms: 

SIP(Smashed-data Inversion based on Pre-trained model) : The attacker trains an inversion model (a GRU-based decoder followed by an MLP projection) on an auxiliary dataset using the pre-trained head model. The inversion model learns to map smashed data (head model activations) back to input token sequences. 

TAG (Text Revealer from Gradients) : TAG reconstructs output labels by matching gradients. The attacker intercepts the gradients flowing back from the tail model to the server. Using the pre-trained tail model, the attacker initializes dummy label logits and iteratively optimizes them to minimize the distance between the dummy gradients (produced by the pre-trained tail on the dummy labels) and the true gradients. The recovered labels in LLM tasks substantially overlap with the input, enabling reconstruction of the private sequence. 

LAMP (Label-extracting Attack with Model Priors) : LAMP extends TAG by incorporating stronger language model priors. Instead of optimizing over continuous logit space, LAMP alternates between (1) discrete token selection based on the current dummy logits and (2) gradient matching optimization. 

CPTA (Connecting Pre-trained Tail Attack) : CPTA directly connects the pre-trained tail model to the server’s output hidden states, bypassing the trained tail model entirely. The attacker simply feeds the intercepted server outputs into the publicly available pre-trained tail model and evaluates its generation against the private labels. 

DualGuard Defense. DualGuard defends against these attacks through two stages: 

Stage 1: Local Warm-Up Parameter Space Transformation. Before formal fine-tuning, a three-layer projection MLP is inserted between the head and tail models. The head, tail, and ProjectionMLP are jointly optimized on the client side via multi-task learning with three objectives: (1) maximizing the SIP inversion loss to disrupt smashed-data reconstruction, (2) maximizing the pre-trained tail’s cross-entropy loss to deactivate gradient matching, and (3) minimizing the standard LM loss to preserve task performance. LoRA adapters are used for parameter-efficient training on the resource-constrained client. The trunk model is not involved in this stage. 

Stage 2: Global Fine-Tuned Parameter Space Retention. During formal split-based fine-tuning, the head model is frozen to maintain the secure forward activations established in Stage 1. LoRA adapters on the server (trunk) and tail models are fine-tuned on the target dataset, while base model weights remain frozen. An anti-pre-trained-tail loss term ( λ3/Lglobal-pt ) is added to prevent the tail from reverting to its vulnerable pre-trained parameter space. In our experiments, we replace the original synchronous training algorithm in Stage 2 with our CommuteProp algorithm.
