ABSTRACT 

Graph neural networks (GNNs) rely on message passing over graph edges, mak-ing their computational and memory costs strongly dependent on graph density. Graph sparsification offers a natural way to reduce these costs, but removing edges indiscriminately can distort important communication structure and degrade pre-dictive performance. We introduce Scaffold , a topology-based, unsupervised graph sparsification framework derived from support graph theory precondition-ers. Scaffold explicitly controls two complementary structural quantities: di-lation , which measures the length of rerouting paths induced by removed edges, and congestion , which measures how strongly these rerouted paths concentrate on the retained support. By jointly controlling dilation and congestion, Scaffold 

preserves short communication paths while avoiding structural bottlenecks. To our knowledge, Scaffold is the first scalable GNN sparsification framework to use a joint supporting-path dilation-congestion criterion. Across 19 homophilic and heterophilic benchmarks spanning small to large graphs, Scaffold achieves the best aggregate rank among the evaluated sparsification and related methods. Using only 10% –50% of the original edges per sparse support, Scaffold re-covers or closely approaches full-graph GNN performance while using less than half the memory of full-graph training and reducing end-to-end training time, in-cluding sparsification overhead. We provide an open-source software package at 

github.com/siddhartha047/Scaffold .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) (Zhou et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2022a) have been remarkably success-ful across a wide range of graph-learning applications. GNNs operate through two iterative steps, 

aggregation and update , where each node first aggregates information from its neighborhood and then updates its embedding using the aggregated information (Hamilton et al., 2017). Graphs used for GNN training are typically stored in sparse formats such as coordinate (COO), compressed sparse row (CSR), or compressed sparse column (CSC). COO stores edges explicitly as source-destination index pairs and is commonly used for edge-wise Gather-scatter operation, where source-node features are gathered along edges and aggregated at destination nodes. CSR/CSC instead store the sparse adjacency matrix in compressed row/column form and support efficient sparse-matrix-dense-matrix multiplication ( SpMM ). These sparse aggregation operations often dominate GNN execution, with SpMM alone accounting for approximately 70 % of the total computation cost (Liu et al., 2023). Since their cost scales with the number of processed edges, GNN training can become expensive on larger and denser graphs (Chen et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2019). A common approach to scaling GNNs is sampling (Hamilton et al., 2017; Chiang et al., 2019), which reduces per-iteration computation by typically controlling the size of the sampled computation graph rather than explicitly preserving its global structure. Graph sparsification offers a complementary structure-aware sampling perspective by selecting a subset of edges while preserving properties of the original graph so that the resulting subgraph remains an effective support for message pass-ing (Spielman & Teng, 2011; Loukas, 2019; Hashemi et al., 2024). However, not all sparsification objectives align with the communication requirements of GNNs. Equally sized sparse graphs can induce very different communication structures, forcing originally adjacent nodes to communicate arXiv:2609.31466v1 [cs.LG] 25 Sep 2026 Scaffold 

through long detours or rerouting many communication paths on a small number of edges or hub nodes. This is particularly important in the unsupervised setting, where labels and downstream task information are unavailable for identifying task-specific edge importance. The overarching goal is therefore to construct sparse graphs that preserve structural properties that support effective com-munication while satisfying a fixed edge budget. Among structural properties, low dilation and low congestion are particularly relevant to sparsi-fication. In support graph theory, dilation measures how far communication is rerouted when an edge is removed, while congestion measures the crowdedness or density of rerouted paths on the retained support. These concepts originate from combinatorial preconditioners to solve systems of linear equations, and were later formalized through support theory and congestion-dilation analy-sis (Vaidya, 1991; Boman & Hendrickson, 2003; Bern et al., 2006; Maggs et al., 2005). Together, they characterize how well a sparse graph supports the original graph. We bring this perspective to graph representation learning through Scaffold , a practical and scalable sparsification frame-work that explicitly controls dilation and congestion under a prescribed edge budget. If removing an edge forces its endpoints to communicate through a long or highly congested supporting path, that edge is poorly represented by the current sparse graph and receives higher priority for retention.

To-gether, these variants support both fixed sparsi-fication and stochastic GNN sampling. These sparse supports also provide practical efficiency benefits. As illustrated in Figure 1 on R EDDIT ,

Our contributions are as follows: (i) We introduce Scaffold , a topology-based unsupervised and theoretically rigorous sparsi-fication framework that translates support-graph dilation and congestion from combinatorial preconditioners to graph representation learning (§2). (ii) We develop a scalable family of Scaffold algorithms with different quality-cost trade-offs, supporting both fixed sparse graphs and stochastic sparse views for efficient GNN training and inference (§3). (iii) We characterize how dilation and edge congestion bound the structural and communication distortion introduced by sparsification, and empirically study their implications for the GNN message passing framework (§3.4). (iv) Across 19 datasets and 20 related baselines, Scaffold achieves strong predictive perfor-mance while reducing memory and end-to-end training cost. With each sparse support retain-ing only 30% and 50% of the original edges, Scaffold reaches within one percentage point of full-graph performance on 12 /19 and 16 /19 datasets, respectively (§5).

All Scaffold variants share three components: a support backbone F, the support score in Eq. 6, and a candidate-selection policy. For feasible budgets, the spanning-forest backbone ensures that every omitted edge has a supporting path, while the selection policy determines how aggressively paths and scores are recomputed. In general, F may be deterministic, randomized, weight-aware, or low-stretch (Abraham et al., 2008; Abraham & Neiman, 2012). For weighted graphs, Scaffold 

uses the supplied edge weights through the path metric. A weighted formulation of Scaffold 

using weighted path lengths and routing loads is given in Appendix A.3. Since all benchmark graphs in this work are unweighted, we use a deterministic spanning forest ( SF ) together with randomized forests and fast low-stretch approximations. These algorithms are novel support-theoretic sparsifiers.

Starting from H0 = F, it recomputes supporting paths, congestion statistics, and candidate scores after every insertion, retains the highest-scoring edge, and repeats until |E H| = q. Our theoretical analysis covers sparse-support properties rather than nonlinear GNN dynamics. In the dilation-only setting, selecting large-detour edges bounds the maximum dilation remaining after sparsification. For an unweighted graph G and a support H ⊆ G preserving its connected compo-nents, dilation and edge congestion yield LH ⪯ LG ⪯  1 + D(H)CE (H)LH (Boman & Hen-drickson, 2003; Bern et al., 2006). This bounds effective-resistance distortion for connected graphs, while separate path arguments bound graph distances and edge-cut sizes. Node congestion gives an analogous neighborhood-concentration bound but is not required by the Laplacian bound. Complete statements, assumptions and proofs are provided in Appendix B. A general approximation guaran-tee for the full dilation–congestion objective remains open, and these structural bounds do not imply monotonic improvement in downstream GNN accuracy. 

Runtime and memory. For an L-layer GNN with hidden dimension d, message passing on a q-edge Scaffold support costs O(Lqd ) per epoch, compared with O(Lmd ) on the full graph, while graph-dependent memory decreases from O(m) to O(q). If one support costs CScaffold to gen-erate, refreshing every ρ epochs gives amortized generation cost CScaffold /ρ . 4 RELATED WORK 

Support-graph theory (Vaidya, 1991; Boman & Hendrickson, 2003; Bern et al., 2006) was devel-oped for combinatorial preconditioners, with support-tree preconditioners and low-stretch spanning trees constructing sparse backbones with short replacement paths (Maggs et al., 2005; Elkin et al., 2005; Abraham et al., 2008; Abraham & Neiman, 2012). Graph spanners (Alth¨ ofer et al., 1990) are closely related to the dilation component of Scaffold , whereas Scaffold follows the broader support-theoretic view by jointly controlling dilation and congestion under a fixed edge budget. To the best of our knowledge, Scaffold is the first GNN sparsifier to explicitly use this joint criterion to construct the graph used directly for graph learning. Broadly, sparsification approaches in GNNs can be grouped into: topology-based sparsifiers using spectral, degree, similarity, or traversal crite-ria (Spielman & Teng, 2011; Spielman & Srivastava, 2008; Hamann et al., 2016; Voudigari et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2007; Satuluri et al., 2011; Leskovec & Faloutsos, 2006), semantic-based methods that learn sparse structures from downstream signals (Chen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024; 2025), and sampling-based methods that reduce the computation graph processed during training (Hamilton et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2019; Chiang et al., 2019). Scaffold is a topology-based approach that scores how well omitted edges are represented by paths in the retained support. A broader discussion of related work is provided in Appendix D.

Datasets and evaluation. We evaluate Scaffold on 19 node-classification benchmarks: eight homophilic, six heterophilic, and five large-scale graphs. All graphs are made undirected and self-loop-free before sparsification. We report Accuracy for single-label classification and ROC-AUC for MINESWEEPER , Q UESTIONS , and OGBN -PROTEINS . Support-score components and structural quality. Figure 9 compares components of Equation 1 under the same support backbone ( SF ) and budget. Prioritizing low dilation improves over random filling ( ∅), which highlights the importance of short communication paths. Edge or node congestion alone can favor longer routes and is less effective, whereas combining dilation and edge congestion gives the strongest predictive association with test performance. Node congestion captures a distinct theoretical failure mode in controlled examples (§A.4), but provides limited additional benefit on these GNN benchmarks. This pattern is not specific to Scaffold . In Figure 10, all sparsifiers share the same spanning forest and edge budget, isolating their edge-selection rules. Methods that better reduce dilation and edge congestion achieve higher GNN accuracy, with dilation showing the strongest association. This may partly explain why Random remains competitive once connectivity is guaranteed, as random edges can shorten detours and partially alleviate structural bottlenecks.

6 CONCLUSIONS 

We introduced Scaffold , a novel unsupervised topology-based graph sparsification framework that uses the support-theoretic notions of dilation and congestion to construct sparse communica-tion graphs for GNNs. Across benchmarks, Scaffold maintains strong performance at low edge budgets while reducing memory and end-to-end training cost. These results indicate that, at a fixed edge budget, how omitted connections are represented by the retained graph matters for GNN per-formance. Our theory characterizes the resulting structural distortion, but a general approximation guarantee for the full dilation-congestion objective remains open. The current evaluation focuses on static, undirected, unweighted node-classification graphs. Extensions of Scaffold to task-aware, localized, and broader graph-learning settings are left for future work. Scaffold

A.2 SUPPORT -T HEORETIC BACKGROUND 

Support graph theory was developed for constructing sparse combinatorial preconditioners for sym-metric diagonally dominant linear systems (Vaidya, 1991; Boman & Hendrickson, 2003; Bern et al., 2006). For graph Laplacians LG and LH, the support number is defined as 

σ(LG , LH) = min {τ : LG ⪯ τ LH},

where smaller τ indicates that the sparse graph H provides a closer spectral support for G.For graph Laplacians, this algebraic relation also admits a combinatorial interpretation. An edge omitted from H is represented by a supporting path in H connecting the same pair of endpoints. The quality of this representation depends on two complementary properties: (i) how long the sup-porting path becomes relative to the omitted edge, and (ii) how strongly supporting paths overlap on the retained graph. These effects are captured by dilation and congestion , respectively. Classical congestion–dilation arguments relate their joint effect to the quality of the support graph (Vaidya, 1991; Boman & Hendrickson, 2003; Bern et al., 2006). 

Scaffold builds directly on this perspective. Rather than using the sparse support H as a pre-conditioner for solving a linear system, we use it as the communication graph for GNN message passing. An omitted edge is therefore well supported when its endpoints remain connected by a short path and when that path does not pass through heavily shared parts of the retained graph. This motivates the dilation–congestion criteria used throughout Scaffold .Scaffold

Equation 1 combines dilation, edge congestion, and node congestion in the Scaffold objective. Figure 12 gives controlled node-classification examples that isolate the three quantities. The source then lies outside the two-layer receptive field, reducing accuracy from 100% to chance. This illustrates finite-hop under-reaching caused by long supporting paths. For edge congestion , the two supports have the same dilation and node congestion, but the detri-mental support routes more omitted relations through a shared edge, increasing CE from 5 to 7.The resulting bottleneck makes the paired targets indistinguishable, whereas separating the routes recovers perfect prediction. For node congestion , dilation and edge congestion remain fixed while the detrimental support routes multiple relations through a common hub, increasing CV from 3 to 12 . The shared intermediate representation causes the targets to become indistinguishable, whereas private relays avoid this node-level bottleneck. These constructions show that dilation, edge congestion, and node congestion capture comple-mentary support-level communication failures, even when their empirical importance differs across benchmark families. B.2 DILATION REDUCTION IS NON -S UBMODULAR 

Proposition B.2 (Dilation reduction is not submodular) For a fixed backbone F, let 

f (S) = D(F) − D(F ∪ S),

where dilation is measured over the edges that remain omitted. Then f is not submodular in general. Proof. Consider three branches 

r − a1 − a2 − a3, r − b1 − b2 − b3, r − c1 − c2,

with candidate edges ea = ( r, a 3), eb = ( r, b 3), and ec = ( r, c 2). Their initial dilations are 3, 3, 2.Let A = ∅, B = {ea}, and e = eb. Adding eb to A leaves ea with dilation 3, so 

f (A ∪ { eb}) − f (A) = 0 .

After ea is already retained, adding eb reduces the maximum remaining dilation from 3 to 2, giving 

f (B ∪ { eb}) − f (B) = 1 .

Hence the marginal gain increases as the selected set grows, violating the diminishing-returns con-dition. Thus, the standard submodular-greedy approximation guarantee does not apply. B.3 GUARANTEE FOR DILATION -O NLY SELECTION

Let q = ⌈δm ⌉. For feasible budgets, each Scaffold variant starts from a spanning-forest back-bone F, with one spanning tree per connected component of G. For a current support H and candidate set B, S UPPORT SCORES (G, H, B) computes sup-porting paths and evaluates Eq. 6, with congestion and normalization computed over B.C.1.1 SUPPORT BACKBONES 

The backbone F may be deterministic, randomized, weight-aware, or low-stretch (Elkin et al., 2005; Abraham et al., 2008; Abraham & Neiman, 2012). Different backbone constructions induce different supporting paths and, consequently, different dilation and congestion profiles. All benchmark graphs in this work are unweighted; weighted extensions are described in Appendix A.3. The subsequent 

Scaffold scoring principle is otherwise unchanged. We use spanning-forest terminology throughout: SF denotes the deterministic construction,

C.4 POSSIBLE EXTENSION : L OCALIZED OR PARTITIONED SUPPORTS 

The current Scaffold formulation uses a spanning-forest backbone so that every omitted edge has a finite supporting path whenever the requested budget is feasible. A possible extension is to apply the same scoring principle locally within graph partitions or clusters, constructing a connected support for each subgraph rather than one global spanning forest. The main difficulty is the treatment of cross-partition edges. If the endpoints of an omitted edge are disconnected in the current support, its supporting-path dilation is infinite and the standard 

Scaffold score is not directly applicable. A localized variant would therefore require an addi-tional mechanism for cross-partition connectivity, such as retaining a sparse global skeleton, explic-itly preserving selected boundary edges, or scoring cross-partition edges separately. We leave the design and analysis of such localized Scaffold constructions to future work. Scaffold 

D APPENDIX RELATED WORK 

Graph sparsification methods differ in the information they use and the properties they aim to pre-serve. Topology methods range from simple degree and similarity heuristics (Hamann et al., 2016; Voudigari et al., 2016; Xu et al., 2007; Satuluri et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2023) to classical construc-tions with explicit structural objectives. Cut sparsification (Bencz´ ur & Karger, 2015) preserves cuts, spectral sparsification (Spielman & Teng, 2011; Spielman & Srivastava, 2008; Batson et al., 2009) preserves Laplacian structure, and graph spanners (Alth¨ ofer et al., 1990) control distance distor-tion. Semantic methods instead use additional information to identify useful sparse structures. Neu-ralSparse (Zheng et al., 2020), SparseGAT (Ye & Ji, 2021), PTDNet (Luo et al., 2021), SGCN (Li et al., 2020), Unified-LTH (Chen et al., 2021), AdaGLT (Zhang et al., 2024), and SGS-GNN (Das et al., 2026) learn graph structure together with downstream training signals, while MoG (Zhang et al., 2025) learns task-dependent routing over multiple sparsifier experts. Sampling methods address a related but different scalability problem. GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2017) samples neighborhoods, FastGCN (Chen et al., 2018) and LADIES (Zou et al., 2019) sample layer-wise computation, GraphSAINT (Zeng et al., 2019) samples training subgraphs, and Cluster-GCN (Chiang et al., 2019) trains on graph partitions. These approaches primarily limit the compu-tation performed in each training iteration rather than construct a single global sparse support. Other related graph-modification approaches include stochastic edge removal with DropEdge (Rong et al., 2019), diffusion-based preprocessing with GDC (Gasteiger et al., 2019), and learned graph struc-tures such as ProGNN (Jin et al., 2020) and NodeFormer (Wu et al., 2022b). Recent work continues to explore GNN sparsification for efficient training and inference (Akkas & Azad, 2025; Liguori et al., 2025; Liao et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2025), alongside broader meth-ods for analyzing and estimating graph sparsification error (Wang & Lopes, 2025). Graph/model co-sparsification and lottery-ticket methods (Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Hui et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024), learned graph structure and neural sparsification (Zheng et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020; Luo et al., 2021; Franceschi et al., 2019; Jin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2023), and classical spectral, cut, and distance-preserving constructions (Bencz´ ur & Karger, 2015; Spielman & Teng, 2011; Spielman & Srivastava, 2008; Batson et al., 2009; Alth¨ ofer et al., 1990) further illustrate the breadth of graph-reduction approaches. Our experiments use represen-tative topology, semantic, and sampling methods rather than attempting an exhaustive comparison across all graph-reduction paradigms. 

Scaffold is a topology method, but differs in how it evaluates an omitted edge: its importance is determined by the path that must support that edge in the retained graph. With dilation alone, this is closely related to distance-preserving sparsification such as graph spanners. Jointly consid-ering dilation and congestion instead follows the support-theoretic view that even short replace-ment paths can create severe bottlenecks when many omitted connections share the same retained edges. To our knowledge, prior GNN sparsifiers have not explicitly used this joint support-theoretic dilation–congestion criterion to construct the graph used directly for message passing. The different 

Scaffold variants realize this criterion with different quality-cost trade-offs.

F.4 CASE STUDY OF PERFORMANCE OF DIFFERENT DATASETS 

The results in Tables 9,10,11 show cases where edge budget alone does not explain performance. 

Budgets below the spanning-forest floor. For sparse graphs such as ROMAN -EMPIRE , CITESEER ,and CORA , some evaluated retention ratios fall below the spanning-forest floor. At these budgets, no method can simultaneously satisfy the edge budget and preserve all connected components, so the resulting degradation should primarily be interpreted as a consequence of an infeasible connectivity budget rather than edge-selection quality. 

Local neighborhood preservation on MINESWEEPER . MINESWEEPER is a notable exception to the aggregate trend: local similarity-based sparsifiers outperform Scaffold -1 at δ = 0 .7. This suggests that, for some tasks, preserving the distribution of retained edges within individual neigh-borhoods can be more important than optimizing global support paths. 

Backbone choice on CHAMELEON and SQUIRREL . These small heterophilic graphs are the two cases where Scaffold -1 uses the low-stretch SLSF backbone. Here Scaffold -1, Spectral, and MoG obtain similar performance, while several degree- or similarity-based sparsifiers are noticeably weaker. This is consistent with the importance of preserving short, well-distributed communication paths on these graphs. 

Scaffold -K is not uniformly better than Scaffold -1. Refreshing supports generally im-proves performance, but the gain is dataset dependent. In particular, Scaffold -K slightly un-derperforms Scaffold -1 on OGBN -PROTEINS , REDDIT , and OGBN -PRODUCTS at the matched target ratios, while improving substantially on datasets such as OGBN -ARXIV and POKEC . Because 

Scaffold -K trains on individual q-edge supports but evaluates on their union, its benefit depends on whether the refreshed supports provide complementary useful structure. Also, irregular swap of support graph can be harmful. The effects of refresh frequency and training versus inference topology are examined further in Appendix H.4. For large graphs, constructing a new sparse support can be expensive and may stall GPU training. 

Scaffold-Sample mitigates this by precomputing edge-sampling probabilities, making subse-quent support sampling inexpensive. Other variants, such as Fast and Batch , can require substan-tially more time to construct each support. We therefore generate the next support asynchronously on CPU workers while the GPU trains on the current one, and swap supports once the new support is ready. As shown in Figure 18, frequent support refresh is generally preferable to long refresh intervals. On Cora, synchronization has little effect on runtime. On the relatively larger A MAZON -COMPUTERS graph, asynchronous generation reduces runtime by 7.9× for Fast and 1.4× for
